Lonja — the app as an authoritative document

A printed regulations booklet bound together with a nineteenth-century ichthyological plate, carried in an oilskin pocket. Twenty screens, one design system, entirely offline. Set in cool bone paper, green-black ink and harbour blue.

Design system · Lonja

The citation is not fine print. It is the design.

Serif for anything with legal weight, sans for chrome, mono for every number a fisher might be asked to defend. Hairline rules, margin article numbers, a footnote rule above every citation. Semantic colour is separate from the accent and is never the only signal.

Every verdict is a statement of fact. No imperative verb appears anywhere in the product: never keep, never return, never throw back.

Icon & stroke

1.45 px stroke, butt caps, mitre joins, no fills, no rounded corners — the vocabulary of an engraver’s burin rather than a UI kit. Species are drawn as plates: outline plus sparse hatching at 0.7 px and 50 % opacity.

Theme policy

The page around the phones is theme-aware; the phone screens are not. The app ships one committed printed palette — bone paper and green-black ink — plus a separate sunlight theme, and it does not invert with the viewer’s operating system, because a fisher’s screen at 05:40 is set by the sun, not by a preference pane.

Three themes, not two. Paper (default) · Sunlight (maximum contrast, monochrome plus the result colour only) · and glove mode, which is a target-size mode layered on either.

Faces. Iowan Old Style / Palatino / Georgia for law and species accounts. System sans for chrome. Mono for codes, dates and measurements. Geeza Pro / Noto Naskh for Arabic. No webfonts — every face is already on the device, which is the only kind of asset an offline app should trust.

Closing argument

Why Lonja

Khalid is not being helped to make a choice — he is being told what the law says, and he may have to repeat it to an inspector an hour later. An interface that looks like an app invites the question “says who?”, whereas one that looks like the gazette it is quoting answers that question before it is asked: the article number is in the margin, the citation sits under a footnote rule, and the date the wording was last checked is printed beside it. The engraved plate at the top of every result does the identification work that a photograph cannot do at 05:40 in a wet bin, and it gives the verdict something to be stamped beneath — a printed judgement rather than a UI card, which is the difference between a document and a notification.

Serif for the law, mono for every number he might have to defend, and a sans reserved for chrome, so that the three kinds of information never blur into one another. The semantic inks — verdant, oxblood, ochre — are printed colours, muted enough to survive glare and distinct enough to read as three different facts, and they are never carried alone: an icon and a plain sentence say the same thing three ways. The one real risk here is committing to a printed aesthetic on a wet phone at dawn; the sunlight theme is the answer, and it is a genuine third theme rather than a dark-mode inversion.
